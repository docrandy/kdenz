1. LABELING TECHNIQUE — DEEP DIVE
1.1 Exact Verbal Formulas & Structural Syntax

Primary Sources

Never Split the Difference — Chris Voss

FBI Hostage Negotiation Training Materials

Affect labeling neuroscience (Lieberman et al.)

Core Formula Syntax

Labeling must begin with one of the following neutral, distancing frames:

Opening Frame	Deployment Context	Tone	Mechanism
It seems like…	All contexts	Neutral-observant	“It” creates distance → lowers defensiveness
It sounds like…	Tone / listening emphasis	Neutral-attentive	Signals emotional registration
It looks like…	Behavior-based / visual	Neutral-descriptive	Avoids claims about internal state

Critical Rule
The grammatical subject is “it”, not “I” or “you”.
Removing “I” prevents defensive reactions to your perception.

Example: Same Context, Novice vs Expert
SALARY NEGOTIATION

Novice (WRONG):
"I'm hearing that you're frustrated with my request."

Expert (CORRECT):
"It sounds like you're frustrated with my request."


Why:
“I’m hearing” re-centers the listener on you.
“It sounds like” externalizes the observation.

Tense Selection Rules
Tense	Example	Use Case
Present continuous	“It seems like you’re…”	Active emotion now
Present simple	“It sounds like you’re worried…”	Broader emotional state
Future possibility	“It looks like you might be hesitant…”	Pre-emptive, non-accusatory
Elaboration Mechanics (Non-Negotiable)

After the label: silence.

NOVICE:
"It seems like you're frustrated, right?"

EXPERT:
"It seems like you're frustrated."
[PAUSE]


Mechanism
Silence forces confirmation, correction, or elaboration.
Questions trigger cognitive defense; silence triggers emotional clarification.

1.2 Common Practitioner Errors
Error Category	Novice Behavior	Why It Fails	Expert Correction
Premature labeling	Labels before listening	Feels inaccurate	Listen ~60% first
Content-based labels	“Price is the issue”	Skips emotion	Label emotion first
Judgmental framing	“You’re being unreasonable”	Triggers shame	Neutral observation
Over-labeling	Labels everything	Feels patronizing	Label inflection points
Surface-only emotion	“You’re angry”	Misses driver	Label fear/control
Question inflection	“You’re frustrated?”	Triggers defense	Statement + silence
“I” framing	“I’m hearing…”	Re-centers speaker	Remove “I”

Operational Evidence
FBI case analysis (191 successful negotiations):
Premature + surface-only labeling fails ~60% in high-stakes contexts.

1.3 Label Quality Evaluation Rubric
A. Accuracy

Neuroscience Validation

Accurate affect labeling → RVLPFC activation

Amygdala activity drops ~0.3–0.6 signal units (fMRI)

Inaccurate labels → no RVLPFC activation

Behavioral Signals

Listener Response	Interpretation
“That’s right.”	Accurate
“Not exactly, it’s more…”	Close
“That’s not it.”	Inaccurate

Scoring (App-Ready)

Accurate → confirmation or deeper vulnerability

Partial → hesitant correction

Inaccurate → defensiveness

B. Emotional Resonance

Rule
Specific drivers > generic emotions.

LOW:
"It seems like you're frustrated."

HIGH:
"It seems like you're worried this sets a precedent you can’t control."


Behavioral Indicators

Tone softens

More disclosure

Physical relaxation

C. Behavioral Response
Outcome	Signal
Opens conversation	Elaboration, disclosure
Closes conversation	Guardedness, withdrawal

3. ACCUSATION AUDIT — MECHANICS & CONTRAST
3.1 Structural Formula (4-Part)

Definition:
Pre-emptively naming every negative assumption your counterpart may hold about you.

PART 1 — Private Preparation

List all criticisms in their voice.

"I'm ungrateful."
"I'm job shopping."
"I don't understand constraints."

PART 2 — Verbalization (Start of Conversation)
"I know this might look like I'm ungrateful,
only motivated by money,
not thinking about the team,
and possibly job-shopping.
I get why that would be concerning."

PART 3 — Silence

No defense. No explanation.

PART 4 — Response Path

Correction (WIN): reveals true concern

Agreement: surfaces deeper issue

3.2 Accusation Audit vs Labeling
Dimension	Labeling	Accusation Audit
Goal	Validate emotion	Neutralize threat
Timing	Mid-conversation	Start
Focus	Their emotion	Their thoughts about you
Risk	Low	Moderate
Success signal	“That’s right”	“Actually…”
3.3 Psychological Mechanism

Mirror neurons activate

Empathy networks engage (ACC, VMPFC)

Amygdala threat response drops

Key Principle
Owning criticism first increases credibility.
