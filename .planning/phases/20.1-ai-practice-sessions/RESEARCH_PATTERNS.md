# Research: Communicator Pattern Taxonomy
*Agent 1 of 5 — Phase 20.1 Research*
*Completed: 2026-02-18*

---

## Summary

Across five major communication frameworks — Voss/FBI negotiation, NVC, Motivational
Interviewing, CBT/Schema Therapy, and Crucial Conversations — the same 7-8 failure-mode
clusters appear with striking consistency. The literature names them differently (MI calls
it the "righting reflex"; NVC calls it "advising as an empathy blocker"; Crucial Conversations
calls it "controlling"; executive coaches call it "the fixer") but they converge on the same
underlying behavioral patterns. This convergence validates building a cross-framework taxonomy
rather than framework-specific pattern sets, and provides strong grounding for each pattern
name and its prescribed intervention.

---

## Cross-Framework Convergence

of human communication, converging from radically different disciplines.

## Framework-by-Framework Source Notes

### Voss / Black Swan Group (Negotiation Training)

**Three negotiator types with distinct failure modes:**

1. **Analyst** — Methodical, data-driven, avoids emotional language. Failure mode: listens for
   logic, misses emotional black swans (fear, status, internal politics). Retreats into data
   under pressure instead of probing discomfort. Gets stuck in information-gathering mode.
   Asks too many questions too early.

2. **Accommodator** — Relationship-focused, creates psychological safety. Failure mode: avoids
   uncomfortable questions (money, authority, risk). Hides objections. Likely to agree to things
   they can't deliver. Optimism without realism.

3. **Assertive** — Time-oriented, direct, pushes hard. Failure mode: fills silence with more
   talking. Talks past subtle signals. Triggers resistance before black swans emerge. Doesn't
   listen to responses.

**Additional Voss failure modes (not type-specific):**
- Pursuing "yes" too early → raises guard, signals manipulation
- Using logic-based arguments when emotional reality is the block
- Not triggering "no" → missing the signal of real engagement
- "Fair" manipulation — conceding without getting information

### NVC (Rosenberg) — Empathy Blockers

Rosenberg catalogued the specific ways people fail to provide empathy (the "roadblocks" or
"empathy blockers"). These are highly specific and observable:

**The complete list:**
1. **Advising** — "I think you should..." / "Why don't you..."
2. **One-upping** — "That's nothing, listen to what happened to me..."
3. **Educating** — "This could turn into a positive experience if you just..."
4. **Consoling / Reassuring** — "It wasn't your fault, you did the best you could..."
5. **Story-telling** — "That reminds me of the time when I..."
6. **Shutting down / Dismissing** — "Cheer up. Don't feel so bad."
7. **Sympathizing** — "Oh, you poor thing..." (pity, not presence)
8. **Interrogating** — "When did this begin? What caused it?"
9. **Explaining / Justifying** — "I would have called but..."
10. **Correcting** — "That's not how it happened."

**NVC's four life-alienating communication categories:**
- Moralistic judgments (right/wrong framing)
- Comparisons ("Why can't you be more like...")
- Demands (vs. requests)
- Denial of responsibility ("I had to," "You make me feel")

### Motivational Interviewing — Counselor Traps

MI is the richest source for this taxonomy because its traps were designed to identify exactly
what practitioners do wrong when they care about helping people:

1. **Righting reflex** — The urge to fix, correct, give the solution. Generates counter-argument
   from the client. The helper argues FOR change; the client argues AGAINST it.

2. **Question-answer trap** — Over-reliance on closed questions creates an interrogation dynamic.
   Client becomes passive recipient. Counselor becomes "expert."

3. **Expert trap** — Assuming you have the answers and the client needs to receive them.
   Contradicts the spirit of collaborative discovery.

4. **Premature focus trap** — Directing toward a specific goal before establishing the relationship
   and before understanding where the client actually is.

5. **Labeling trap** — Forcing diagnostic labels on someone before they're ready to accept them.
   Generates shame and defensiveness.

6. **Taking sides trap** — Getting into an argument about who is right. Immediately loses the
   collaborative frame.

7. **Cheerleading trap** — Excessive positive reinforcement that doesn't match the client's
   internal experience, creating disconnect.

8. **Pouncing trap** — Jumping on the first sign of change talk before it's genuinely developed.

**The righting reflex is the master failure mode.** MI frames this as the natural human impulse
to help by telling people what to do — but it backfires because it puts the helper in the expert
role and the other person in the passive role, which reduces their agency and triggers resistance.

### CBT / Schema Therapy — Communication Coping Styles

CBT schema therapy identifies three coping styles that show up in communication:

1. **Surrender** — Accepting the maladaptive schema as accurate and acting accordingly.
   In communication: gives in, defers, doesn't advocate for themselves.

2. **Avoidance** — Avoiding situations that activate the schema. In communication: sidesteps
   difficult topics, doesn't engage fully, withdraws.

3. **Overcompensation** — Doing the opposite of what the schema would suggest. In communication:
   overasserts, controls, explains too much, corrects others.

**Schemas most relevant to communication patterns:**
- **Approval-seeking** — "I need people to approve of me." Observable: constant validation-seeking,
  hedging, softening statements, adding "right?" to the end of assertions.
- **Self-sacrifice** — "I put others' needs before mine." Observable: unable to state direct needs,
  excessive accommodation, difficulty saying no, agrees to things they disagree with.
- **Emotional inhibition** — "Showing feelings leads to bad consequences." Observable: stays in
  cognitive/analytical mode, avoids emotional language, intellectualizes, struggles to name
  feelings in self or others.
- **Defectiveness/shame** — "I am fundamentally flawed." Observable: over-explains to justify
  existence, defensive pre-emptive explanations, over-apologizes.

**CBT communication-specific patterns:**
- Stage-hogging / Narcissistic listening: not hearing others, waiting to speak
- Ambushing: listening to find ammunition for attack, not understanding
- Selective listening: only processing information that confirms existing view
- Pseudo-listening: appearing to listen, not actually processing

### Crucial Conversations — Styles Under Stress

The SUS (Style Under Stress) framework maps 6 patterns across two dimensions:

**Silence (withholding meaning):**
1. **Masking** — Sugarcoating, sarcasm, indirect communication. Understating true opinion.
2. **Avoiding** — Steering away from sensitive topics entirely while appearing to engage.
3. **Withdrawing** — Exiting the conversation or physically leaving.

**Violence (forcing meaning):**
4. **Controlling** — Dominating through cutting off, overstating, absolute statements.
5. **Labeling** — Dismissing ideas/people with stereotypes to end engagement.
6. **Attacking** — Moving beyond argument to making the person suffer. Belittling, threatening.

### Executive Coaching Frameworks

Multiple practitioner frameworks converge on 4 archetypes:

1. **The Authority / Controller** — Driven, goal-oriented, takes charge. Failure: people stop
   speaking up; doesn't realize they're not listening. Intervention: shift from action-planning
   to active listening.

2. **The Reactionary** — Committed, quick decision-maker, passionate. Failure: half-listens,
   responds before understanding, creates unnecessary drama. Intervention: consciously slow down
   before responding.

3. **The Avoider / People-Pleaser** — Emotionally attuned, popular. Failure: avoidance turns
   small issues into major problems; signals weakness. Intervention: build confidence through
   planned interactions.

4. **The Fixer** — Helpful, jumps in without being asked. Failure: removes others' agency,
   causes burnout. Intervention: ask permission before helping.

## Pattern Taxonomy — Final Draft

Based on cross-framework synthesis, 8 patterns account for the vast majority of communication
failure modes observed across all frameworks. Each name is user-facing and growth-mindset framed.

---

### Pattern 1: The Surface Reader

**User-facing name:** Surface Reader
**Growth-edge reframe:** "You're ready to go deeper."

**What they do (observable signals):**
- Responds to the literal content of what was said, not the subtext beneath it
- Misses emotional shifts, hesitations, or signals of underlying concern
- Asks information-gathering questions (who, what, when) rather than emotional-processing questions
- Gives competent, factually correct responses that land wrong
- Often leaves others feeling "handled" rather than heard

**Root cause:** Optimizes for information processing; treats conversations as data exchange.
Emotional subtext is not visible as a data type.

**What it costs them:** Others don't feel understood. Trust stays at surface level. In
negotiation/conflict, they miss the real driver and get stuck at position level forever.

**Cross-framework grounding:** Voss Analyst blindspot; NVC "interrogating + analyzing" empathy
blockers; MI "question-answer trap"; CBT "emotional inhibition" schema.

**Prescribed intervention:** Subtext reveal drills (what was beneath their words?); labeling
practice (naming the feeling/need beneath the statement); spot-the-technique exercises on
transcripts where subtext is highlighted.

**Institute content type:** "Before/after" transcript comparisons showing surface vs. subtext
response. Short-form videos demonstrating what a labeling response sounds like versus an
analytical response to the same trigger.

---

### Pattern 2: The Hedger

**User-facing name:** Hedger
**Growth-edge reframe:** "Your instincts are good. Learn to land them."

**What they do (observable signals):**
- Adds "I think," "maybe," "kind of," "perhaps," "sort of" to accurate observations
- Ends statements with "right?" or "does that make sense?" seeking validation
- Softens reflections until they lose impact ("It sounds like you might be feeling...
  possibly... a little frustrated?")
- Corrects themselves mid-sentence when they were right the first time
- Backs down when pushed even if their read was accurate

**Root cause:** Approval-seeking schema; fear that being wrong about someone's emotional state
will damage relationship. Uncertainty becomes a defense mechanism.

**What it costs them:** The hedge closes the space they just opened. A perfect label followed by
"right?" invites the other person to reject it. The signal of uncertainty becomes the signal they
send — not confidence, empathy, or competence.

**Cross-framework grounding:** NVC "consoling/reassuring" as avoidance of direct empathic
presence; CBT approval-seeking schema; Voss Accommodator blindspot (hides real read to preserve
rapport); MI "cheerleading trap" (over-qualifying to avoid confrontation).

**Prescribed intervention:** Label delivery drills — deliver the label clean, no hedge, hold
silence. Score on whether the hedge was added. Silence-holding exercises (count to 4 after
labeling, don't fill the space).

**Institute content type:** Side-by-side audio comparison — hedged label vs. clean label, same
scenario. Article on why certainty in empathy is a gift, not arrogance.

---

### Pattern 3: The Fixer

**User-facing name:** Fixer
**Growth-edge reframe:** "You're capable. Now let them be capable too."

**What they do (observable signals):**
- Pivots from empathy to solution within 1-2 exchanges
- Offers advice before fully understanding the other person's experience
- Jumps to problem-solving mode as soon as a problem is named
- Their responses start with "What you should do is..." or "Have you tried..."
- Treats emotional expression as a preamble to be gotten through quickly

**Root cause:** MI's "righting reflex" — the genuine, well-intentioned impulse to help by
fixing. High confidence in their own competence combined with discomfort sitting with
someone else's pain without doing something about it.

**What it costs them:** Generates resistance. The other person argues against the solution
(even if it's good) because their experience hasn't been validated first. Trust erodes
because the person feels like a problem to be solved, not a human to be heard.

**Cross-framework grounding:** MI "righting reflex" (most important single concept in MI);
NVC "advising/correcting/educating/fixing" empathy blockers (4 of Rosenberg's 10 roadblocks);
executive coaching "Fixer" archetype; Crucial Conversations — often manifests as "controlling."

**Prescribed intervention:** Empathy-before-advice drills. Score sessions on how many empathy
turns occurred before any solution attempt. Technique: "Name three feelings before naming one
option." Exercises in holding curiosity rather than deploying competence.

**Institute content type:** Video demonstrating the righting reflex in action with annotated
analysis of what happened. Article: "Why the best help is often no help." Exercises: role-play
where advice is literally forbidden for 5 exchanges.

---

### Pattern 4: The Diplomat

**User-facing name:** Diplomat
**Growth-edge reframe:** "Your warmth is an asset. Now add your honesty."

**What they do (observable signals):**
- Avoids conflict and difficult topics at the cost of honest communication
- Sugarcoats, softens, or finds indirect routes to hard truths
- Agreement comes before genuine alignment ("Yes, totally" followed by invisible objection)
- Hides their real read to preserve relationship harmony
- Often described by others as "agreeable" but privately frustrated with outcomes

**Root cause:** Self-sacrifice schema + fear of conflict. The belief that harmony is more
valuable than clarity. Conflict feels like relationship damage.

**What it costs them:** Small issues become large ones (avoidance). Others can't trust their
"yes" because it doesn't reveal their real state. In negotiation, they concede without knowing
it and then feel resentful. The relationship they tried to protect often suffers anyway.

**Cross-framework grounding:** Voss Accommodator blindspot (avoids discomfort, hides objections);
NVC "consoling/sympathizing/dismissing" empathy blockers; MI avoidance patterns; Crucial
Conversations "Masking," "Avoiding," "Withdrawing" styles; executive coaching "Avoider/People-Pleaser."

**Prescribed intervention:** Calibrated question practice — asking hard questions from curiosity
rather than aggression. "No-oriented question" drills (Voss: "Would it be crazy to...?"). Exercises
in naming the actual tension rather than smoothing it. Accusation audit practice (naming the
discomfort before it names you).

**Institute content type:** Article on the cost of false harmony. Video: what tactical empathy
sounds like when you're about to say something the other person doesn't want to hear. Exercises
in assertiveness using NVC request framework.

---

### Pattern 5: The Intellectualizer

**User-facing name:** Intellectualizer
**Growth-edge reframe:** "You think clearly. Now feel clearly too."

**What they do (observable signals):**
- Responds to emotional situations with analysis, logic, and frameworks
- Talks about feelings rather than expressing or receiving them ("They were probably
  experiencing cognitive dissonance about...")
- In emotionally charged moments, produces accurate observations delivered with clinical distance
- Can explain what the other person is probably feeling without ever connecting with it
- Others describe them as "brilliant but cold"

**Root cause:** Emotional inhibition schema (CBT). Feelings were unsafe or unrewarded; cognition
was rewarded. Emotional language feels performative or vulnerable.

**What it costs them:** The "accurate but cold" problem. They are often right about what's
happening but create no emotional resonance. Others don't feel met. Advanced techniques like
labeling become tools of analysis rather than tools of connection.

**Cross-framework grounding:** CBT emotional inhibition schema; NVC "analyzing/interpreting/
diagnosing" empathy blockers (3 of Rosenberg's roadblocks); Voss Analyst blindspot (listens
for logic, misses emotional black swans); executive coaching "Analyzer" type.

**Prescribed intervention:** Emotion vocabulary drills — naming feelings with emotional
language, not analytic language. Practice using first-person emotional language ("I notice
I want to...") before labeling others' states. Exercises where analytical responses are
flagged and replaced with emotionally present ones.

**Institute content type:** Video demonstrating the difference between accurate-but-cold and
accurate-and-warm responses to the same scenario. Article on the neuroscience of emotional
resonance. NVC feelings vocabulary as a reference resource.

---

### Pattern 6: The Presumptuous One

**User-facing name:** Presumptuous
**Growth-edge reframe:** "You read people well. Now check your read."

**What they do (observable signals):**
- States interpretations as facts ("You're clearly upset about X")
- Frames observations as certainties rather than hypotheses ("The real issue is...")
- Corrects others' own account of their experience
- Proceeds based on their assumption without verifying it
- Can be right 70% of the time, but the 30% creates ruptures

**Root cause:** High pattern-recognition ability without the corresponding verification habit.
Overconfidence in the accuracy of first-read. Often reinforced by history of being right.

**What it costs them:** When wrong, they create defensive walls. The label that should open a
door closes it. Others feel misread, categorized, and unable to correct the record.

**Cross-framework grounding:** NVC "diagnosing/labeling" empathy blockers; MI "labeling trap"
(forcing labels before the other person is ready); Crucial Conversations "Labeling" violence
pattern; CBT mind-reading cognitive distortion.

**Prescribed intervention:** Mirroring + verification drills — state observation as hypothesis,
not fact. "It seems like..." / "I'm wondering if..." constructions. Spot-the-technique exercises
where presumptuous vs. tentative framings are compared. Exercises in holding interpretation
loosely and testing it before acting on it.

**Institute content type:** Before/after comparisons: presumptuous framing vs. hypothetical
framing of the same observation. Article on why tentative labeling is more powerful than
certain labeling. Video demonstrating what it sounds like when someone checks their read.

---

### Pattern 7: The Rusher

**User-facing name:** Rusher
**Growth-edge reframe:** "You can move fast. Now move at their pace."

**What they do (observable signals):**
- Fills silence immediately; doesn't let responses land
- Multiple-question sequences without waiting for answers
- Moves to the next topic before the current one is resolved
- Pace of exchange is faster than the emotional processing of the other person
- Often time-oriented; treats conversation as a task to complete

**Root cause:** Assertive pattern (Voss); time-oriented listener style; sometimes anxiety
about silence creating discomfort. High internal processing speed creates mismatch.

**What it costs them:** The most powerful moments in conversation require silence. Labels,
mirrors, calibrated questions — all rely on holding space AFTER deployment. The Rusher
deploys the technique correctly and then immediately undermines it by filling the silence.

**Cross-framework grounding:** Voss Assertive blindspot (fills every silence, talks past subtle
signals); MI "pouncing trap" (jumps on first change-talk before it develops); Crucial
Conversations "Controlling" pattern; time-oriented listener type in interpersonal communication
research.

**Prescribed intervention:** Silence-holding drills. Count to 5 (minimum) after every label,
mirror, or calibrated question. Exercises where the score includes silence duration as a metric.
Pace-awareness training with audio playback to hear where they rushed.

**Institute content type:** Audio demonstration: same exchange at two different paces, with
annotation showing where the opening appeared and was missed. Article on why silence is
productive. Video on tactical use of "let that land."

---

### Pattern 8: The Explainer

**User-facing name:** Explainer
**Growth-edge reframe:** "You're clear in your thinking. Now trust your reader."

**What they do (observable signals):**
- Over-justifies positions, requests, or actions with excessive context
- Pre-emptively explains their reasoning before it's been challenged
- Adds "because..." to simple statements that don't require justification
- When challenged, explains more rather than asking what was heard
- Statements grow longer under pressure, not shorter

**Root cause:** Approval-seeking schema + defectiveness/shame schema in combination.
Over-explaining is a trauma-informed behavior rooted in environments where actions were
constantly questioned. The belief: "I must justify my existence in this conversation."

**What it costs them:** Explanations invite debate. Every "because" is an implicit invitation
to evaluate whether the reason is good enough. In negotiation, explaining your position gives
the other side information and reduces leverage. In conflict, long explanations are read as
defensiveness.

**Cross-framework grounding:** NVC "explaining/justifying" empathy blocker; CBT approval-seeking
and defectiveness schemas; Voss — over-explanation as signal of diminished leverage; Crucial
Conversations "Masking" (sugarcoating).

**Prescribed intervention:** Calibrated question substitution — replace explanations with
questions. "What's making that feel like too much?" instead of three sentences justifying
why your request is reasonable. Drilling statement economy — same idea, half the words. Audio
playback to hear where justification began.

**Institute content type:** Transcript exercise: identify where explanation started and what
question could have replaced it. Article on the negotiation cost of over-explaining. Video
demonstrating the power of silence after a request (no explanation required).

## Naming Principles

The naming challenge is real: pattern names must feel like self-identification, not diagnosis.
The following principles emerged from the research:

**1. Noun phrases over adjective phrases**
"Surface Reader" not "Surface-Level Listener." The name is an identity, not a judgment.
"The Hedger" not "The Uncertain Communicator."

**2. The name describes what they DO, not what's wrong with them**
"Rusher" describes observable behavior. "Anxious Communicator" describes pathology.
"Fixer" describes a strategy. "Conflict-Avoidant" describes a diagnosis.

**3. Names should feel like recognition, not correction**
When someone hears "Surface Reader," they should think "yes, that's exactly what I do"
not "that's bad." The name should explain behavior they've already noticed, in a way that
makes them curious rather than defensive.

**4. Each name has an implicit compliment**
- Surface Reader → you notice things; you just haven't gone deeper yet
- Hedger → you have good reads; you just don't trust yourself to land them
- Fixer → you're capable and want to help; that's a strength to channel
- Diplomat → your warmth is real; your honesty needs to join it
- Intellectualizer → you think clearly; now bring your emotional intelligence online
- Presumptuous → you read people fast; now check your reads
- Rusher → you move efficiently; now move at their pace
- Explainer → you're clear in thinking; now trust the other person to keep up

**5. Avoid clinical language entirely**
No: "avoidant," "anxious," "defensive," "narcissistic," "passive-aggressive"
Yes: "Diplomat," "Hedger," "Fixer," "Rusher"

**6. The growth-edge reframe is part of the pattern definition**
"You're ready to go deeper" (Surface Reader) — the name points forward, not back.
The pattern name + growth-edge reframe IS the debrief. Not "you have this problem" but
"here's where you are and here's the natural next step."

## Implementation Notes for Planning

### Detection Signal Priority

For Gemini pattern detection (transcript-based), patterns rank by detection ease:

**High confidence detection from transcript alone:**
- Hedger: "I think," "maybe," "kind of," "right?", "does that make sense?" — high precision
- Fixer: "What you should do," "Have you tried," solution within 2 exchanges — high precision
- Explainer: statement length under pressure, "because" frequency — medium-high precision
- Presumptuous: "You clearly," "The real issue is," declarative framing — high precision

**Medium confidence detection from transcript:**
- Rusher: silence duration requires audio; exchange pace detectable from turn length patterns
- Intellectualizer: vocabulary analysis (cognitive vs. emotional language), medium precision
- Surface Reader: requires comparing response to what was available in subtext — needs rubric

**Lower confidence from transcript, higher with audio:**
- Diplomat: requires inferring what was NOT said; easier with multi-session behavioral data
- Rusher: silence duration is the key signal — needs audio metadata from Phase D

### Session Pattern Confidence Threshold

Do not assign a pattern name after 1 exchange. Gemini should track signal frequency:
- 1 exchange: note signal, do not label
- 2-3 exchanges: tentative pattern note ("consistent hedging observed")
- 4+ exchanges with consistent signals: assign pattern name for debrief

### Multi-Session Pattern Persistence

After 3 sessions, pattern should stabilize. After 10 sessions, look for shift. The debrief
framing should change: "You've been a Hedger in our last 3 sessions. This week, I noticed you
landed two labels clean. You're moving."

### Pattern ↔ Technique Mapping (for Institute routing)

| Pattern | Primary Technique Deficit | Prescribed Drill Type |
|---------|--------------------------|----------------------|
| Surface Reader | Labeling (subtext) | Spot-the-Technique, Subtext reveal |
| Hedger | Label delivery, silence-holding | Prompt-Response (scored on hedge presence) |
| Fixer | Tactical empathy before advice | Prompt-Response (advice forbidden) |
| Diplomat | Calibrated questions, accusation audit | Rewrite drills |
| Intellectualizer | Emotion vocabulary, presence | Audio-Spoken (emotional response scored) |
| Presumptuous | Mirroring, hypothesis framing | Prompt-Response (certainty flagged) |
| Rusher | Silence deployment | Audio-Spoken (silence duration scored) |
| Explainer | Calibrated question substitution, economy | Rewrite drills |

### Institute Tagging — Pattern Field Values

The `addresses_patterns[]` field in Institute content items should use these canonical string IDs:
```
"surface-reader" | "hedger" | "fixer" | "diplomat" | "intellectualizer" |
"presumptuous" | "rusher" | "explainer"
```

### Pattern Convergence Risk

Some users will exhibit multiple patterns. Suggested handling:
- Primary pattern = the one with highest signal frequency in session
- Secondary pattern = second most frequent, noted but not the lead label
- Debrief addresses primary pattern; Institute routing considers both
