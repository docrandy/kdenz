Short synthesis first, then each of your numbered questions.

**High‑level synthesis for your model**

Across COM‑B, behavioral economics, and ACC‑centric neuroscience, there
is a fairly stable pattern:

- **Objective task demands** (distance, rain, force, time, scheduling,
  clutter) are treated as *environmental or task features*---i.e.,
  *inputs into* the decision system.

- The brain's control/valuation circuitry (ACC, striatum, etc.)
  *transforms those inputs into subjective effort and opportunity
  costs*, and integrates them with benefits.

- "Hard" constraints are typically modeled as **feasibility constraints
  on the choice set** (you literally cannot pass); "soft" constraints
  are **cost terms** in the value function (you can pass, but it is
  expensive).

- COM‑B explicitly defines **opportunity** as environmental
  preconditions and treats capability/opportunity as "logic gates" that
  must be open before motivation is even allowed to generate behavior.
  Within that, friction like rain or distance is usually coded as
  **physical opportunity**, with its unpleasantness/cost entering
  **motivation**.[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

So in a two-level model like yours, "environmental friction" belongs
most cleanly on the **INPUT** side (opportunity / task demands) but its
*effect* is expressed via **PROCESSING constraints** (how strongly costs
are computed and weighted). Your "max motivation" test is therefore not
aligned with COM‑B, and is too coarse for distinguishing friction from
genuinely impossible constraints.

**(1) COM‑B: where does environmental friction live?**

**Core COM‑B definitions**

Michie et al. define the core COM‑B components
as:[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)

- **Capability**: psychological and physical capacity, including
  knowledge and skills.

- **Opportunity**: "all the factors that lie outside the individual that
  make the behaviour possible or prompt
  it."[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)

- **Motivation**: "all those brain processes that energize and direct
  behaviour," including reflective and automatic
  processes.[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)

They further subdivide:[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)

- **Physical opportunity**: "physical opportunity afforded by the
  environment" (time, resources, locations, cues, physical environment).

- **Social opportunity**: norms, interpersonal influence, culture.

In later clarifications, opportunity is defined ontologically as an
**"environmental disposition that is required for or facilitates a
behaviour"**, with *physical opportunity* covering "time and parts of
the environmental system that do not involve people or
organisations."[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

Critically, West & Michie emphasize that **capability and opportunity
function as logic gates**, constraining the relationship between
motivation and behaviour:

> "...they act like 'logic gates' in that both of the 'gates'
> (capability and opportunity) need to be open for motivation \[to\]
> generate the behaviour... If either capability or opportunity are not
> present, **no matter how strong the motivation, it will not
> happen**."[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

So within COM‑B, *opportunity is not something that can be "overridden"
by enough motivation*; it is a necessary precondition.

**How would COM‑B classify your examples?**

Given those definitions and their worked examples, the standard COM‑B
reading would
be:[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)

- **Rain making running unpleasant**

  - The *meteorological condition* (rain, slippery pavement, cold) is
    clearly **physical opportunity**: features of the environment that
    "facilitate or are required for" the action but in this case make it
    more aversive and risky.

  - The *feeling of aversion* ("I don't want to get soaked") sits in
    **automatic motivation**---emotional response and habit.

  - COM‑B explicitly allows opportunity to change motivation
    (opportunity → motivation arrow). Rain is an opportunity factor
    *that shifts motivation*.[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

- **Distance to the gym, lack of transport**

  - Distance, travel time, absence of public transport or car =
    paradigmatic **physical opportunity** constraints.

- **Messy room**

  - The clutter is part of the **physical environment**, so again
    **physical opportunity**.

  - The feeling of overwhelm or disgust at the mess is **automatic
    motivation**, and any explicit goal ("I should clean this") is
    reflective motivation.

- **Inconvenient timing / scheduling conflicts**

  - COM‑B includes *time* explicitly under **physical opportunity**. Not
    having a free half hour is lack of opportunity, not lack of
    motivation.[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

  - Feeling too tired after work, or preferring Netflix, is
    motivational; but the calendar itself is opportunity.

So COM‑B does **not** reclassify these frictions as "motivation" simply
because they can be overcome. They are almost always treated as
**opportunity constraints that shape motivation**. Interventions aimed
at reducing these frictions (e.g., moving the gym closer, altering
schedules, providing equipment at home) are labeled **environmental
restructuring / enablement** in the Behaviour Change Wheel, i.e., they
target
*opportunity*.[^[\[3\]]{.underline}^](#fn3)[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)

For your model: COM‑B implicitly answers your core question by saying:

- Rain/distance/messiness/timing are still **external, input‑side
  constraints (Opportunity)**.

- Their influence is *mediated* by motivational processes, but they are
  not redefined as "just motivation" because willpower can sometimes
  override them.

**(2) Behavioral economics & effort discounting: structure vs effort vs
opportunity cost**

**How models distinguish the three**

Across Kool & Botvinick's labor--leisure work and Westbrook & Braver's
effort‑discounting frameworks, you can map:

1.  **Structural barriers**

    - These are **hard constraints on the choice set** or "budget
      constraint": outcomes or action sequences that are simply not
      available.

    - In Kool & Botvinick's labor--leisure paradigm, the worker has a
      budget of hours per week; choices outside that budget are
      infeasible.[^[\[4\]]{.underline}^](#fn4)

    - In a literal locked‑door case, this is modeled as
      **zero‑probability transitions** or actions not present in the
      action set, not as a large cost.

2.  **Effort costs**

    - Can pass but requires expenditure (e.g., more demanding cognitive
      task, more lever presses, higher force, longer duration).

    - **Formally represented as a cost term in the utility function or
      discount function**, e.g. cognitive effort discounting (COGED) or
      physical effort discounting.

    - Westbrook et al. (2013) use cognitive load (N‑back level) as the
      *objective task demand* and measure how much additional money is
      required for people to accept that load; subjective value falls
      with load.[^[\[5\]]{.underline}^](#fn5)

    - In Kool & Botvinick's preferred labor--leisure account, effort
      enters as a **disutility that increases with labor**, and people
      choose an income--leisure balance that maximizes joint
      utility.[^[\[4\]]{.underline}^](#fn4)

3.  **Opportunity costs**

    - "What else could I have been doing with this time/effort?"

    - In the opportunity‑cost account of mental effort (Kurzban et al.;
      extended by Westbrook & Braver), the *felt* cost of effort is the
      **opportunity cost of allocating scarce control/working‑memory
      resources to one task rather than
      others**.[^[\[6\]]{.underline}^](#fn6)

    - Shenhav et al.'s EVC model explicitly treats this as part of the
      "Cost(signal)" term in Eq. 1, which is subtracted from expected
      payoffs.[^[\[7\]]{.underline}^](#fn7)

**Environment vs agent in these models**

The standard decomposition is:

- **Environment / task defines the demand**

  - Required number of N‑back updates, frequency of task switching,
    number of lever presses, physical force, distance, etc. are treated
    as *objective features of the task*---i.e.,
    **inputs**.[^[\[6\]]{.underline}^](#fn6)[^[\[4\]]{.underline}^](#fn4)

- **Agent's valuation system transforms those inputs into subjective
  costs**

  - Westbrook & Braver explicitly stress that cognitive effort is a
    **"subjective, psychological phenomenon"**---a *phenomenal*
    representation of opportunity costs that must be measured via
    revealed preference, not inferred from objective difficulty or
    physiology.[^[\[6\]]{.underline}^](#fn6)

  - Their discounting and COGED paradigms show large individual
    differences and trait effects (Need for Cognition), even when
    objective load is held
    constant.[^[\[5\]]{.underline}^](#fn5)[^[\[6\]]{.underline}^](#fn6)

So:

- **Structural barriers** = properties of the **environment/state
  space** (what actions or state transitions are available); these
  usually are *not* parameterized as costs.

- **Effort and opportunity costs** = internal computations in the
  agent's **valuation function**, driven by external task parameters but
  *not reducible* to them.

For your model, this gives a clean mapping:

- **Input constraints**: things that shape the feasible set and/or the
  mapping from actions to effort demands (distance, rain intensity,
  lever‑press number).

- **Processing constraints**: how the brain *values* those demands
  (steep vs shallow effort discounting, high vs low opportunity‑cost
  sensitivity).

**(3) Neuroscience of effort: input or computation?**

The effort‑based decision‑making literature is remarkably consistent
with the above decomposition.

**ACC and the Expected Value of Control**

Shenhav, Botvinick & Cohen's EVC theory explicitly formulates ACC's role
as computing:

$\text{EVC(signal, state)} = \sum_{i}^{}\mspace{2mu} P(\text{outcome}_{i} \mid \text{signal},\text{state}) \cdot \text{Value}(\text{outcome}_{i})\  - \ \text{Cost(signal)}$[^[\[7\]]{.underline}^](#fn7)

- The **"Cost(signal)" term is the cost of cognitive control / effort**,
  assumed to increase with control
  intensity.[^[\[7\]]{.underline}^](#fn7)

- The **state** includes environmental and internal conditions (task
  difficulty, reward stakes, fatigue, etc.). Task demands such as Stroop
  conflict, load, or physical effort are part of the **state input**.

- dACC is proposed to **integrate rewards and costs** to select the
  control policy with maximal EVC and set its
  intensity.[^[\[7\]]{.underline}^](#fn7)

This is squarely a **computation‑side** account: task demands are
*inputs*; effort cost is a *computed term* within ACC's value function.

**Neuroeconomic evidence**

Westbrook & Braver's neuroeconomic review argues that:

- The subjective cost of effort is encoded in **valuation networks**
  (ACC, ventral striatum, insula), not in early sensory or purely motor
  representations.[^[\[6\]]{.underline}^](#fn6)

- Cognitive effort discounting paradigms (COGED) show that BOLD in ACC
  and striatum tracks the *subjective value* of effortful options
  (reward discounted by effort), not just raw load or
  time.[^[\[6\]]{.underline}^](#fn6)

- Opportunity‑cost accounts link effort signals to striatal dopamine and
  average reward rate (e.g., Niv et al. 2007), again pointing to
  **valuation circuitry**.[^[\[6\]]{.underline}^](#fn6)

On the physical side, motor‑effort studies show:

- In "Neural signatures of value comparison in human cingulate cortex
  during decisions requiring an effort--reward trade‑off," human dorsal
  ACC activity reflects the **difference in subjective value** between
  options after integrating reward magnitude and physical effort in a
  concave effort‑discounting
  function.[^[\[8\]]{.underline}^](#fn8)[^[\[9\]]{.underline}^](#fn9)

- Supplementary motor area and vmPFC separately track pure effort and
  pure reward parameters, while **ACC carries the integrated
  cost--benefit signal** that predicts
  choice.[^[\[9\]]{.underline}^](#fn9)[^[\[8\]]{.underline}^](#fn8)

- Animal work on effort‑related choice (e.g., Salamone et al. 2018)
  assigns **effort costs** to mesolimbic dopamine and nucleus accumbens
  circuits that integrate reward and required work; lever‑press
  requirements are environmental, but costs are internal
  computations.[^[\[10\]]{.underline}^](#fn10)

So empirically:

- **Effort demands themselves are encoded in input representations**
  (e.g., sensory cues, motor parameters, conflict signals).

- **Effort costs are computed in valuation/control circuits** (ACC,
  striatum) as part of the *processing* architecture.

For your framework, it is neurally natural to treat effort demand
*parameters* as **input constraints**, with effort cost as a
**processing constraint** (the brain's way of transforming those
constraints into behavior).

**(4) "Hard" vs "soft" constraints in the literature**

There is no single standardized hard/soft terminology across
behavior‑change frameworks, but there are close analogues:

**In COM‑B and behavior change**

- COM‑B implicitly distinguishes:

  - **No opportunity / no capability** → behavior cannot occur,
    regardless of motivation (e.g., no gym exists, you cannot walk, no
    legal
    access).[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

  - **Limited or costly opportunity** → behavior is possible but
    infrequent or unpleasant (rain, distance, high prices, inconvenient
    hours).

- Their "logic gate" language makes **absence of
  opportunity/capability** a **hard constraint** in your terms: you
  cannot "will" your way past it.[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

- Frictions like mess, distance, time, clutter are treated as **partial
  opportunity deficits** and **targets for environmental restructuring
  and enablement** (e.g., moving services closer, altering
  schedules).[^[\[3\]]{.underline}^](#fn3)[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)

**In economics and RL/control**

- **Hard constraints** = constraints on the **feasible set** (budget
  constraints, physical impossibilities, state/action prohibitions).
  Violating them is impossible.

- **Soft constraints** = things you *can* violate or overcome, but at a
  cost (penalties in the objective function, extra terms in the utility
  or loss).

This maps cleanly to your distinction:

- **Hard constraints**

  - Locked door; no gym in the region; zero public transport; severe
    physical disability.

  - In COM‑B: *opportunity or capability = 0* → no behavior, regardless
    of motivation.

  - In econ/RL: actions or transitions are removed from the choice set
    (or have zero probability).

- **Soft constraints**

  - Rain, distance, paying a fee, standing in line, messiness, timing
    conflicts.

  - In COM‑B: they sit in **physical opportunity**, influencing
    motivation but not replacing
    it.[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

  - In econ/ACC models: they appear as **effort and opportunity cost
    terms** in the valuation function (EVC cost, effort discounting).

Thus, the literature broadly supports:

- Hard/soft is a difference in **how the environment constrains**
  behavior (impossible vs dispreferred),

- but **both** are still **environmental / task constraints** in origin.
  Soft constraints do *not* become "motivation" just because they can be
  overcome; they are **environmental constraints that enter valuation as
  costs**.

**(5) COM‑B: distance to gym and lack of transport**

Within COM‑B's own examples and definitions:

- **Lack of transportation / 30‑mile distance to the gym**

  - Distance, travel time, cost of fuel/public transport, lack of bus
    routes, etc. are **physical opportunity** constraints---features of
    the environment that are "required for or facilitate" the behavior
    and are currently missing or
    weak.[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)

- **Capability** would be implicated if the person:

  - Physically cannot walk/drive,

  - Lacks the skills or knowledge to use available options (e.g., cannot
    navigate public transport, doesn't know alternative forms of
    exercise).

Michie et al. are explicit that COM‑B is meant to be used
**diagnostically**, and that single barriers often map to multiple
components (e.g., capability, opportunity, and motivation). So for your
gym scenario, a COM‑B analysis might
say:[^[\[3\]]{.underline}^](#fn3)[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)

- **Primary**: Physical opportunity (gym far away, limited transport).

- **Secondary**:

  - Psychological capability (not knowing home‑based exercise options).

  - Motivation (valuing exercise insufficiently to justify long trips).

But the *fact that the gym is 30 miles away* is not recoded as
capability or motivation. It is an **environmental constraint** (INPUT)
that reduces opportunity and thereby modulates motivation.

**Implications for your "max motivation" test**

**Why "can maximal motivation overcome it?" is misaligned with COM‑B**

COM‑B is quite explicit that **no amount of motivation can compensate
for zero capability or zero opportunity**. If there truly is no gym and
no alternative means of exercising, then behavior cannot occur in the
COM‑B sense.[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

For frictions like rain, distance, and messiness:

- These do **not** remove opportunity entirely; they **degrade** it and
  simultaneously make the behavior less attractive (costlier).

- Your "if max motivation can overcome it" test therefore conflates:

  - True absence of opportunity (hard constraint),

  - Versus *low‑quality opportunity plus high internal cost* (soft
    constraint).

In COM‑B terms:

- Rain, distance, mess are **opportunity factors** (input constraints)
  that alter both:

  - The *probability* that the opportunity gate is open at the moment
    behavior is cued (e.g., you happen to have free time and dry
    clothes), and

  - The *motivational balance* between action and inaction (cost/benefit
    ratio).

**A more precise test for your behavioral model**

A classification more consistent with the literatures you cited would
look something like:

1.  **Hard environmental constraint (INPUT: 0 feasibility)**

    - Even if motivation and capability were arbitrarily high, **the
      probability of success is effectively zero** (no path exists in
      the state space).

    - Examples: locked door with no key, no gym or safe walking space
      anywhere, the person is completely paralyzed, legal prohibition
      enforced with certainty.

2.  **Soft environmental constraint (INPUT: positive feasibility, high
    demand)**

    - There exists at least one action path from current state to goal,
      but it carries **non‑trivial effort or opportunity
      costs**---greater distance, adverse weather, mess, scheduling,
      fees, travel time.

    - Motivation *can* shift whether that path is chosen, but the
      environmental variable still lives on the INPUT side (it sets the
      slope of the cost function, not the existence of the path).

3.  **Processing constraints (COMPUTATION: how costs are evaluated and
    control allocated)**

    - How strongly effort and opportunity costs are weighted
      (steep/shallow effort discounting),

    - Trait cognitive motivation (Need for Cognition), dopaminergic
      function, ACC valuation parameters,
      etc.[^[\[5\]]{.underline}^](#fn5)[^[\[7\]]{.underline}^](#fn7)[^[\[6\]]{.underline}^](#fn6)

On this view:

- **Environmental friction remains an INPUT constraint**---part of the
  state that defines either feasibility or the "price" of actions.

- **Motivation and control processes determine whether that constraint
  is accepted or avoided**---the COMPUTATION side.

You can still preserve a binary axis (hard vs soft) but it should be
about **feasibility vs cost**, *not* about whether "max motivation" can
do the job.

**How this maps back onto your INPUT vs PROCESSING distinction**

Putting it all together:

- **INPUT constraints (body/environment)**

  - Hard: no path (locked door, no facility, physical impossibility, law
    with near‑certain enforcement).

  - Soft: environmental friction and demand parameters (distance, rain,
    mess, timing, lack of prompts, cost, travel time) that shape effort
    and opportunity costs.

- **PROCESSING constraints (brain evaluative system)**

  - Effort discounting and opportunity‑cost computations (Westbrook &
    Braver; Westbrook et
    al.),[^[\[5\]]{.underline}^](#fn5)[^[\[6\]]{.underline}^](#fn6)

  - Control allocation per EVC (ACC integrating benefits and control
    costs),[^[\[7\]]{.underline}^](#fn7)

  - Trait and state factors (fatigue, dopamine, values) that shift how
    strongly those costs are weighted.

This aligns with:

- COM‑B's placement of friction in **Opportunity**, feeding into
  Motivation.[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

- Behavioral‑economic treatment of effort as **subjective cost** over
  and above objective task
  demand.[^[\[4\]]{.underline}^](#fn4)[^[\[5\]]{.underline}^](#fn5)

- Neuroscience evidence that **cost is computed in valuation circuitry
  given environmental
  inputs**.[^[\[8\]]{.underline}^](#fn8)[^[\[7\]]{.underline}^](#fn7)[^[\[6\]]{.underline}^](#fn6)

So for your model, the clean move is:

- Treat environmental friction as **environmental INPUT constraints that
  parameterize effort and opportunity costs**,

- Treat "willpower" and effort avoidance/acceptance as **PROCESSING
  constraints**---the internal function that maps those inputs into
  behavior.
