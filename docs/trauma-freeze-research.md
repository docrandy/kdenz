**In neuroscience, the defense cascade (amygdala → periaqueductal gray →
freeze/tonic immobility) is triggered by threat detection and overrides
voluntary motor control. My question is about the nature of this
override: Does the trauma-related freeze response operate as a
pre-volitional input constraint --- meaning it bypasses the
goal-evaluation system entirely and blocks motor output before any
cost-benefit computation occurs --- or does it operate within the
evaluative system as an extreme cost-aversion response, where the brain
computes that the anticipated cost (re-traumatization) exceeds tolerance
and outputs \"don\'t act\"? Specifically: (1) Does the amygdala-PAG
freeze pathway receive input from prefrontal cost-evaluation circuits
(dACC, anterior insula, dlPFC) before activating, or does it fire
independently of them? (2) Can sufficient motivation override a trauma
freeze, or is it motivation-invariant like a structural/biological
constraint? (3) What does the latency data show --- does freeze onset
occur faster than cortical evaluation could complete? Cite primary
neuroscience sources, especially LeDoux (2000, 2015), Mobbs et al.
(2007, 2015), Kozlowska et al. (2015), and any recent work on defense
cascade timing relative to prefrontal processing.**

At the level of fast defensive circuitry, the trauma‑related freeze
response is best thought of as a **pre‑volitional constraint implemented
by subcortical "survival circuits"** (in LeDoux's terminology) that can
be engaged before cortical cost--benefit evaluation is complete.
However, these circuits are *not* completely insulated from valuation:
they are rapidly modulated by cortical networks (vmPFC, aMCC/dACC,
anterior insula, dlPFC) that encode threat imminence and expected cost,
and that can both prime freeze and help terminate it. So the answer is
"hierarchical": initial triggering is largely bottom‑up and
pre‑volitional; maintenance, strength, and switching are shaped by
ongoing evaluative
processes.[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)[^[\[3\]]{.underline}^](#fn3)

Below, each of your three sub‑questions is addressed explicitly.

**1. Does the amygdala--PAG freeze pathway get prefrontal
cost‑evaluation input before it activates, or does it fire
independently?**

**Circuit architecture**

Across species, defensive freezing is implemented by a conserved
circuit:

- **Threat detection / valuation (subcortical):** lateral/basolateral
  amygdala (LA/BLA), medial amygdala (MeA), ventromedial hypothalamus
  (VMH), premammillary nucleus.[^[\[3\]]{.underline}^](#fn3)

- **Output for freezing:** central amygdala (CeA) → **ventrolateral PAG
  (vlPAG)** → brainstem premotor nuclei, spinal cord, cerebellar
  vermis.[^[\[4\]]{.underline}^](#fn4)[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

- **Physiology of freeze:** high muscle tone with motor output "brake" +
  parasympathetically driven bradycardia via dorsal motor nucleus of the
  vagus.[^[\[5\]]{.underline}^](#fn5)[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

LeDoux's rodent work shows that both unconditioned and conditioned
threats reach **CeA → PAG** via thalamus--amygdala pathways with very
short latencies, and that vlPAG activation is sufficient to generate
freezing even in decerebrate animals. Kozlowska et al. explicitly
characterize the defense cascade (arousal → freeze → fight/flight →
tonic immobility) as **"innate, hard‑wired, automatically activated
defense behaviors"** mediated by extended
amygdala--hypothalamus--PAG--brainstem
circuits.[^[\[5\]]{.underline}^](#fn5)[^[\[3\]]{.underline}^](#fn3)

**Evidence for independence of prefrontal input at onset**

**Timing data** show that subcortical threat processing precedes
prefrontal engagement:

- In rodents, auditory threat signals reach LA from the thalamus in \~12
  ms, and from cortex only later; LA--CeA--PAG circuitry can generate
  freezing via this "low road" without cortical
  participation.[^[\[3\]]{.underline}^](#fn3)

- In humans, MEG and intracranial EEG show:

  - Thalamus/hypothalamus gamma ERS at **10--20 ms** and amygdala at
    **20--30 ms** in response to fearful
    faces.[^[\[6\]]{.underline}^](#fn6)

  - Amygdala gamma peaks for fearful expressions at \~100--135 ms, for
    gaze at \~200 ms, for full faces \~250
    ms.[^[\[7\]]{.underline}^](#fn7)

  - Prefrontal cortex (IFG, ACC/OFC) shows onset around **170--210 ms**,
    clearly *after*
    amygdala.[^[\[6\]]{.underline}^](#fn6)[^[\[7\]]{.underline}^](#fn7)

These latencies imply that **initial amygdala activation is not
contingent on prior prefrontal evaluation**. In LeDoux's terms, survival
circuits compute stimulus significance ("is this dangerous?") using
subcortical routes that can bypass cortex; conscious feelings and
reflective evaluations are constructed later in higher‑order cortical
networks.[^[\[8\]]{.underline}^](#fn8)[^[\[3\]]{.underline}^](#fn3)

Similarly, PAG‑mediated effects on motor circuits can be extremely
rapid. In rats, vlPAG stimulation drives cerebellar climbing fibres with
\~15 ms latency and increases α‑motoneuron excitability in concert with
freezing behavior, even under anesthesia. This again indicates that the
core amygdala--PAG--spinal machinery operates at a timescale not
compatible with prior dACC/dlPFC
evaluation.[^[\[4\]]{.underline}^](#fn4)

**But there is prefrontal input, and it matters**

Anatomically, there are **strong top‑down projections** from medial and
orbital PFC (including vmPFC/perigenual ACC and parts of dACC/MCC) to
both amygdala and PAG:

- Tracing work in primates shows vmPFC and orbitomedial PFC projecting
  to specific longitudinal columns of the PAG, as well as dense
  projections to BLA and
  CeA.[^[\[9\]]{.underline}^](#fn9)[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)

- Roelofs' "Freeze for action" review emphasizes that **flexible
  shifting between freezing and active fight/flight "relies on
  fronto--amygdala connections"**, with perigenual ACC and vmPFC
  projections to CeA/PAG enabling release of the vlPAG
  brake.[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

- Mobbs et al. (2007) show that as a virtual predator approaches,
  activity **shifts from vmPFC to PAG**, and interpret this as a
  forebrain--midbrain switch wherein vmPFC/BLA appraise options at
  distal threat, but CeA/PAG dominate responses as threat becomes
  proximal.[^[\[9\]]{.underline}^](#fn9)

More recent human work generalizes this: dynamic threat‑avoidance and
reward‑seeking tasks show **ramping activity in dorsal anterior insula
and anterior midcingulate cortex (aMCC)** together with PAG and bed
nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST) as threat or reward becomes
imminent. These regions encode **imminence and arousal**, not just
simple valence, and thus look like cost/threat monitors feeding into
survival circuits.[^[\[10\]]{.underline}^](#fn10)

In parallel, value‑based escape paradigms (Qi et al. 2018) show that
**"cognitive fear" circuits** (vmPFC, PCC, hippocampus, BLA) are
preferentially engaged when there is time for strategic decision‑making,
whereas **"reactive fear" circuits** (PAG, CeA, hypothalamus, MCC)
dominate when rapid flight is required.[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)

**Synthesis for Q1:**

- The **first wave** of freeze‑related activation in amygdala and vlPAG
  is largely **independent of prefrontal cost‑evaluation circuits** in
  the sense that it can be triggered via thalamic/brainstem routes
  before dACC/dlPFC have time to compute explicit costs and goals.

- However, **very quickly (150--300 ms onward)**, dACC/MCC, anterior
  insula, vmPFC, and dlPFC are engaged, signal threat imminence and
  expected cost, and **modulate** survival circuits via their
  projections to amygdala and
  PAG.[^[\[10\]]{.underline}^](#fn10)[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)[^[\[6\]]{.underline}^](#fn6)

- In LeDoux's framework, survival circuits perform nonconscious
  "significance computations"; prefrontal circuits implement slower,
  more flexible cost‑benefit and goal evaluation that can bias or
  override survival‑circuit
  output.[^[\[11\]]{.underline}^](#fn11)[^[\[3\]]{.underline}^](#fn3)

So the trauma‑related freeze uses a **bottom‑up trigger with top‑down
modulation**, rather than being a pure product of the cortical valuation
system itself.

**2. Can sufficient motivation override a trauma freeze, or is it
motivation‑invariant?**

The answer depends on **timescale** and **threat regime**.

**Experimental evidence: freezing is not motivation‑invariant**

Several human paradigms show that freezing (measured as bradycardia +
reduced body sway) is **sensitive to task context and value**, which
argues against a strictly motivation‑invariant motor block:

- Under threat of shock, subjects show stronger fear‑bradycardia and
  postural freezing when they are **armed and can act** (shoot/no‑shoot
  task) than when they are helpless. Freezing increases as the moment of
  required action approaches, and greater PAG activity during freezing
  predicts faster subsequent responses.[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

- Lojowska et al. show that threat‑induced bradycardia is associated
  with **enhanced detection of low‑spatial‑frequency (coarse) visual
  features** at the expense of detail, consistent with a state optimized
  for rapid, action‑relevant perception ("better safe than
  sorry").[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

- In approach--avoidance tasks with mixed money and shock outcomes,
  stronger freezing‑related bradycardia is linked to more systematic,
  value‑sensitive avoidance choices, and recent computational work shows
  that freezing states modulate how reward and threat magnitudes are
  weighted in approach--avoidance
  arbitration.[^[\[12\]]{.underline}^](#fn12)[^[\[13\]]{.underline}^](#fn13)

In Qi et al.'s FID paradigm, **slow‑attacking predators** (time to plan)
engage vmPFC/PCC/hippocampus, and subjects' flight distances scale with
both reward level and shock level---i.e., with explicit cost‑benefit. In
**fast‑attacking conditions**, "reactive fear" circuits (PAG, MCC)
dominate, but even here PAG and insula responses are parametrically
modulated by shock magnitude, indicating graded cost sensitivity inside
the reactive circuit.[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)

So in non‑traumatic laboratory settings, freezing is **highly state‑ and
value‑dependent**, integrating both bottom‑up threat signals and
top‑down task demands.

**Clinical and peritraumatic freeze**

For **peritraumatic tonic immobility** (rape, assault, combat), the
picture is different:

- Kozlowska et al. emphasize that tonic immobility is a **"strategy of
  last resort"**, mediated by phylogenetically old brainstem/vlPAG
  circuits that can be elicited even in animals without a cerebrum, and
  that subjects commonly experience these states as "overwhelming and
  beyond conscious control."[^[\[5\]]{.underline}^](#fn5)

- Tonic immobility can be induced by restraint + fear in decerebrate
  animals, showing that the basic shutdown program does not require
  cortical motivation or cost computation at
  all.[^[\[5\]]{.underline}^](#fn5)

- In humans, peritraumatic reports frequently describe intense wish to
  act (run, fight, scream) coupled with inability to move or speak. This
  phenomenology is consistent with **reactive survival circuits
  overriding cortical intentions**.

At the same time, trauma history and trait anxiety clearly modulate
freezing propensity:

- Individuals with prior trauma show **stronger freezing‑like reductions
  in sway and stronger bradycardia** to aversive images and social
  threat cues.[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)[^[\[5\]]{.underline}^](#fn5)

- Early‑life attachment insecurity predicts stronger bodily freezing in
  adolescence.[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

- PTSD patients in some paradigms show **reduced freezing** to
  unpleasant pictures (and more hypervigilant scanning), suggesting
  maladaptive shifts between defensive modes rather than uniformly
  exaggerated freeze.[^[\[14\]]{.underline}^](#fn14)

**Role of prefrontal "motivation" in override**

At the circuit level:

- vmPFC and perigenual ACC have inhibitory control over amygdala/PAG and
  are critical for extinction, stressor controllability, and switching
  between defensive
  modes.[^[\[9\]]{.underline}^](#fn9)[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

- vmPFC stimulation in rodents can suppress brainstem stress responses;
  perceived controllability recruits mPFC to damp dorsal raphe/PAG
  output.[^[\[9\]]{.underline}^](#fn9)[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)

- In humans, greater vmPFC engagement is linked to larger safety buffers
  and less panic‑like action, whereas reduced vmPFC and heightened PAG
  are seen in panic and chronic
  anxiety.[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)[^[\[9\]]{.underline}^](#fn9)

Putting this together:

- **On short timescales (tens to hundreds of ms)** at high threat
  imminence, reactive circuits can transiently **override goal‑directed
  systems**, making the freeze relatively motivation‑invariant "in the
  moment."

- **On slower timescales (hundreds of ms to seconds)**, **motivation,
  controllability, and goals**---implemented via vmPFC, dACC/MCC,
  anterior insula, dlPFC---*do* influence whether freezing is
  maintained, whether it flips into flight/fight, and how quickly that
  happens.[^[\[10\]]{.underline}^](#fn10)[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

- **Across development**, repeated experience (e.g., trauma, training)
  shifts the thresholds and gains of these circuits, changing how easily
  freeze is triggered and how much prefrontal control is available.

So in a trauma‑related freeze episode, subjective "motivation to act"
can be very high but still **arrive too late and through too weak a
channel** to immediately overcome a strongly engaged vlPAG brake.
However, with training and therapeutic modulation of
prefrontal--amygdala--PAG circuitry, the probability and duration of
freezing can be reduced, which argues against a strictly hard‑wired,
motivation‑invariant constraint.

**3. Latency: does freeze onset precede cortical evaluation?**

**Subcortical latencies**

Rodent and human data converge:

- **Rodents (LeDoux):** auditory threat signals reach LA via thalamus in
  about **12 ms**, and the LA--CeA--PAG pathway can elicit defensive
  responses at comparably short delays.[^[\[3\]]{.underline}^](#fn3)

- **PAG--cerebellar--spinal loop:** Koutsikou et al. show vlPAG
  stimulation evokes climbing fibre responses in cerebellar pyramis at
  \~15 ms and increases α‑motoneuron excitability tightly linked to
  freezing behavior.[^[\[4\]]{.underline}^](#fn4)

- **Humans:** MEG/EEG and intracranial EEG:

  - Hypothalamus/thalamus gamma ERS at **10--20 ms**; amygdala ERS at
    **20--30 ms** to fearful faces, i.e., *before* visual cortex (40--50
    ms) and long before PFC.[^[\[6\]]{.underline}^](#fn6)

  - Amygdala gamma peaks for fearful expressions at \~100--135 ms; for
    gaze/face information at \~200--250 ms.[^[\[7\]]{.underline}^](#fn7)

  - Prefrontal regions (IFG, ACC, OFC) show onset \~170--210 ms and peak
    \~220--250 ms.[^[\[6\]]{.underline}^](#fn6)

Given these timings, **the earliest activation of amygdala--PAG survival
circuits precedes classical prefrontal evaluation by at least \~100 ms**
and in some paradigms by \~150--200 ms.

**Behavioral freeze latencies vs cognitive evaluation**

Overt freezing as measured in humans---bradycardia and reduced
sway---develops over several hundred milliseconds to seconds after
threat cues in picture‑viewing and threat‑of‑shock paradigms. By that
point, some cortical processing is certainly
ongoing.[^[\[5\]]{.underline}^](#fn5)[^[\[2\]]{.underline}^](#fn2)

However:

- The **initial "brake" signal from vlPAG to motor systems** can be set
  up extremely quickly (tens of ms in animals; plausibly \<200 ms in
  humans) based on subcortical evaluation.

- **Cortical cost--benefit evaluation**, particularly the kind involving
  dACC/dlPFC conflict monitoring and explicit trade‑offs, typically
  unfolds in the 200--600 ms window and beyond, and in decision tasks
  reaction times are on the order of \>500 ms.

Mobbs' threat‑imminence work fits this: during distal threat, vmPFC/BLA
are active and likely evaluating alternative actions; as threat becomes
proximal, there is a **shift to PAG‑dominated activity** and vmPFC
activity diminishes, consistent with midbrain circuits taking over when
"a fast and indeed obligatory response is required." Qi et al. show that
when attack is fast (little time), reactive circuits (PAG/MCC) dominate
escape decisions; when attack is slow, cognitive circuits
(vmPFC/hippocampus/PCC) have time to optimize
behavior.[^[\[9\]]{.underline}^](#fn9)[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)

Thus, **freeze onset at the neural level clearly occurs before full
cortical goal evaluation could complete**. What cortical evaluation does
is:

- modulate the **likelihood** that subcortical circuits cross threshold
  (e.g., via prior expectations, controllability, learned value), and

- influence the **duration and transformation** of the freeze state
  (e.g., facilitate shift to fight/flight vs. prolonged immobility).

**Bringing it back to your two conceptual options**

In the terms you proposed:

1.  **Pure pre‑volitional input constraint (bypass of goal
    evaluation):**

    - The **initial triggering** of trauma‑related freeze by the
      amygdala--vlPAG circuit is indeed **pre‑volitional and can occur
      via subcortical routes** with latencies too short for full
      cortical cost--benefit
      computation.[^[\[7\]]{.underline}^](#fn7)[^[\[3\]]{.underline}^](#fn3)[^[\[6\]]{.underline}^](#fn6)

    - This circuit behaves like an **intrinsic constraint**: if threat
      salience crosses a certain threshold in sensory/survival circuits
      (especially in contexts made hyper‑sensitive by prior trauma), PAG
      outputs can suppress ongoing motor programs regardless of current
      goals.

2.  **Extreme cost‑aversion within the evaluative system:**

    - Over slightly longer windows (hundreds of ms to seconds),
      **cortical evaluative networks (vmPFC, dACC/MCC, anterior insula,
      dlPFC)** interact bidirectionally with survival circuits,
      computing threat imminence, expected pain, controllability, and
      reward trade‑offs, and these signals modulate freezing, escape, or
      fight.[^[\[15\]]{.underline}^](#fn15)[^[\[10\]]{.underline}^](#fn10)[^[\[1\]]{.underline}^](#fn1)

    - In chronic trauma and anxiety, these evaluative systems may be
      biased such that **anticipated re‑traumatization cost is
      over‑weighted**, lowering the threshold for PAG‑mediated
      inhibition and making freeze more likely and more persistent in
      ambiguous situations.

Empirically, the system looks like a **two‑tier architecture** (very
close to LeDoux's "two‑system" and Mobbs' "cognitive vs reactive fear"
frameworks):

- A **fast, largely pre‑volitional survival circuit** (thalamus/sensory
  → amygdala → hypothalamus/PAG/brainstem) that implements basic
  defensive modes, including freezing and tonic immobility.

- A **slower, flexible evaluative circuit** (vmPFC, dlPFC, dACC/MCC,
  insula, hippocampus, BLA) that learns, predicts, and arbitrates
  between options, and exerts top‑down control over survival circuits
  when time and controllability permit.

Trauma‑related freeze is therefore **not simply a calculated "don't act"
from the goal system**, but neither is it a completely sealed‑off spinal
reflex. It is a **pre‑volitional survival constraint whose thresholds
and release are shaped by motivational and evaluative circuitry**, both
acutely and over development.
